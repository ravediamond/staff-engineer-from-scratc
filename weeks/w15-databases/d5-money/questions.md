# Questions

1. Why a double-entry, append-only ledger instead of a `balance` column? What do you gain and what does it cost?
2. Design the idempotency of `POST /transfers`: key, storage, scope, expiry, and the race between two concurrent retries.
3. Your service calls a payment provider and the response times out. What state are you in, and what do you do?
4. Ticket booking for a 50,000-seat concert that sells out in a minute: the hard part and your design for it.
5. What does reconciliation catch that tests cannot? How often, and what happens with a difference?
6. Where do you need a distributed transaction, and what do you use instead (saga, outbox, TCC)?

## Model answers

1. A balance column says what is, not why: a bug or a race silently corrupts it and nothing can prove or repair it. An append-only ledger of balanced entries is the audit trail: every balance is the sum of immutable facts, the invariant "all entries sum to zero" is checkable at any time, corrections are explicit reversal entries, and history as of any date is free. Cost: balances must be derived (cache them in a balance table updated in the same transaction, or snapshot periodically), more rows, and a discipline (no updates or deletes, enforced by permissions).
2. The client generates a UUID per intended operation and sends it in an `Idempotency-Key` header. The server stores (key, tenant, request fingerprint, status, response) with a UNIQUE constraint, in the same database transaction as the ledger entries. First request: insert the key (in progress), do the work, store the response. Retry with the same key and fingerprint: return the stored response. Same key, different fingerprint: 422. Two concurrent retries: the unique constraint lets one insert win; the other waits or gets 409 and retries. Scope keys per tenant/endpoint; expire after 24 h to days, longer than any client retry window.
3. Unknown: the charge may or may not have happened. Never treat a timeout as failure and never blindly retry without the provider's idempotency key. Persist the payment as PENDING before calling, with your own idempotency key sent to the provider; on timeout, query the provider by that key (or retry with the same key, which is safe); a background job resolves PENDING payments, and provider webhooks are a second signal (themselves deduplicated). Only then move to SUCCEEDED or FAILED and write the ledger entries.
4. Contention on a small set of rows and fairness under a huge burst, with no oversell. A waiting room / queue in front to admit users at the rate the system can serve. Seat inventory in one strongly consistent store, partitioned by event or section; `hold` as an atomic conditional write (`UPDATE seats SET holder, expires WHERE id = ? AND (free OR expired)` or a Redis `SET NX PX` backed by the database as the source of truth); short TTL; payment during the hold; `confirm` as a conditional transition checked against the database clock; expiry releases seats back. Idempotency keys on hold and confirm. Read paths (seat map) served from a cache that may be slightly stale; the conditional write is the only arbiter.
5. Everything outside your code: provider-side failures and duplicates, lost webhooks, manual operations, currency and fee differences, timing cut-offs, and your own bugs that are consistent with your own data. Run at least daily against settlement files (and continuously against webhooks for large systems). Each difference becomes a tracked case with an owner: auto-resolve known classes (timing), alert on the rest, and fix with ledger entries, never with edits.
6. Whenever one business operation spans systems with no shared transaction: your database and a provider, two services, a database and a broker. Two-phase commit across them is rarely available and blocks on coordinator failure. Instead: saga (a sequence of local transactions with compensating actions, orchestrated by a durable workflow or choreographed by events); outbox for "update my database and publish an event"; TCC (try-confirm/cancel: reserve, then confirm or release: holds are exactly this). All rely on idempotent steps and on designing for the intermediate states being visible.
