# Questions

1. How do you choose a partition key and a partition count? What breaks when you add partitions later?
2. What exactly does the idempotent producer guarantee, and what does it not?
3. What triggers a consumer-group rebalance, and what did KIP-848 (GA in Kafka 4.0) change?
4. A handler takes minutes per message. Give three designs that work and the trade-off of each.
5. What is consumer lag, how do you alert on it, and how do you autoscale on it?
6. Consumer groups vs share groups (KIP-932): what does each guarantee, and when do you pick the second?

## Model answers

1. Key = the unit that must stay ordered (order id, user id, device id); nothing coarser than needed, or hot keys overload one partition. Count ≥ max(target throughput ÷ measured per-partition produce throughput, target ÷ measured per-consumer throughput) with headroom for growth, because parallelism of a group is capped by it. Adding partitions changes `hash(key) % n` for most keys: new events of a key land on a different partition than its old ones, so per-key order is broken across the change and keyed state (stream-processor stores, compacted topics) is split. Over-provision at creation, or migrate to a new topic and switch consumers at a clean cut.
2. No duplicates and no reordering caused by producer retries, per partition, within one producer session (PID + per-partition sequence numbers; default since Kafka 3.0). Not covered: duplicates when the application itself sends twice or restarts and re-sends (new PID), and anything on the consumer side. Atomic writes across partitions and consume-process-produce need transactions (`transactional.id`), and effects in external systems need idempotent sinks.
3. A member joins, leaves, crashes (session timeout), or exceeds `max.poll.interval.ms`; subscription or partition-count changes. Classic protocol: stop-the-world: every member revokes everything and the group leader (a client) computes the assignment. KIP-848: the broker-side coordinator computes assignments and reconciles them incrementally: only the partitions that move are revoked, no global pause, and no client-side assignor to keep compatible.
4. (a) Hand off: poll loop gives messages to a worker pool, `pause()`s the partition, keeps polling for liveness, commits when workers finish: keeps ordering if one worker per partition, more code. (b) Make the message a pointer to a job in a real job system (the W5 scheduler: leases, retries, DLQ) and commit immediately: best for very long work, ordering is lost. (c) Share group: per-record acknowledgement, acquisition lock renewed while working, many consumers per partition: simple and scalable, no ordering. Raising `max.poll.interval.ms` alone only makes failure detection slower.
5. Log end offset minus committed offset, per partition, per group. Raw counts mislead (a batch topic has huge harmless lag), so alert on time lag (age of the oldest unprocessed record) against the pipeline's freshness SLO, and on lag that keeps growing over a window. Autoscale consumers on lag (KEDA's Kafka scaler) up to the partition count; beyond that, more partitions or faster handlers.
6. Consumer group: each partition has exactly one owner in the group, records are processed in offset order per partition, progress is one committed offset per partition; parallelism ≤ partitions; a slow record blocks its partition. Share group: many consumers fetch from the same partition, each record is individually acquired (time-limited lock), acknowledged, released or rejected, with redelivery and a delivery-count limit; no ordering guarantee. Pick it for task-queue workloads: independent jobs, slow or uneven handlers, more workers than partitions. Production-ready since Kafka 4.2 (February 2026).
